## The method, in one paragraph

Every self-learner who ends up genuinely fluent (not just exam-certified) runs two tracks **in parallel from day one**: structured study (grammar + SRS vocab/kanji) for accuracy, and comprehensible immersion (listening/reading real-ish Japanese) for the intuitive, native-speed processing that lets you actually watch anime or read a novel without translating in your head. Do only the first and you'll pass exams but freeze up on real content. Do only the second and you'll acquire slowly with no way to patch gaps. The ratio just shifts over time — heavy structure early, heavy immersion later.

## First, a correction that changes your whole target

You said 90–95% marks for N5/N4/N3 — worth knowing the JLPT doesn't actually grade on a percentage. It's pure pass/fail, and you have to clear **two** bars: total score above the pass mark, *and* every individual section above its own minimum (fail Listening alone and the whole test fails, whatever your total is). The actual pass bar is only ~44–53% overall. So "90–95%" isn't the exam's ask — it's you deciding to aim for near-total mastery instead of the bare minimum, which is exactly right if the goal is *real ability*. Concretely: **90%+ raw accuracy on every section of full, timed, official-format mock exams, across 2–3 consecutive attempts** — not one good run.

## Phase 0 — Kana (your literal starting point)

Learn hiragana with a mnemonic method (Tofugu's free guide is the standard — a little image/story per character, way faster than rote memorization), drill daily with a quiz tool until instant, then repeat for katakana. Don't lean on romaji past week one.
**Gate to move on:** you can read any kana — including きゃ, ぱ, ヴ combinations — in 1–2 seconds, in random order, no sounding out. Realistically 1–2 weeks.

## Phase 1 — N5 Foundations

- **Grammar spine:** [Tae Kim's Guide](https://guidetojapanese.org/learn/) (completely free, explains grammar as Japanese logic rather than translated English) alongside **Cure Dolly's "Japanese From Scratch"** YouTube series — same philosophy, video form. (Small note: Cure Dolly's creator passed away in 2021, but the ~90-video series is still up and still one of the most-recommended free resources out there.) Watch a video, then read the matching Tae Kim section.
- **Vocab/kanji engine:** Install **Anki** (free), turn on the **FSRS** scheduler (`Deck Options → Advanced → Optimizer`) — it's Anki's new algorithm and cuts your daily review load ~20-30% for the same retention. Import **Kaishi 1.5k**, the deck that's replaced the old Core 2k/6k as the community-standard starter (1,500 words, native audio, pitch accent, clean sentence cards, free on [GitHub](https://github.com/donkuri/kaishi)/AnkiWeb). Start at 10-15 new cards/day — seriously, don't start higher.
- **Immersion, already:** Watch **Comprehensible Japanese** (Yuki, [cijapanese.com](https://cijapanese.com)) — she speaks *only* Japanese from video one using pictures/gestures. - **Reading:** Skim an [NHK Easy News](https://www3.nhk.or.jp/news/easy/) headline daily (renamed NHKやさしいことばニュース in 2024, same site) — furigana toggle, real news in simplified Japanese.

Push grammar further into Tae Kim, cross-referencing anything unclear in **Imabi** ([imabi.org](https://imabi.org/)) — a genuinely massive, still-actively-updated, 400+ lesson free reference that's about as deep as free Japanese grammar resources get (no audio though, so it's a reference, not a course). Get a genkouyoushi sheet (free printable Japanese grid paper — [incompetech.com has a generator](https://incompetech.com/graphpaper/genkoyoushi/)) and start handwriting kanji. **Gate:** comfortable with Tadoku Level 2, 90%+ every section on N4 mocks.

Imabi becomes your primary grammar source now, not just a reference — N3 is where near-synonym grammar distinctions start mattering. Add **JLPT Tango N3**. This is also your entry into actual native reading — and here's the one thing I'd flag as a correction to what you might expect: **Manga Plus is not it.** It's Shueisha's official app, but it's an English-translation platform for overseas readers — no raw Japanese mode. What you actually want are the publishers' own Japanese platforms: **Shonen Jump+, Magazine Pocket (Kodansha), Comic DAYS, Sunday Webry (Shogakukan), GANMA!** — all free, official, legal, and 100% original Japanese text (first/latest chapters of most series, no login wall for those). Pair this with **jpdb.io** (free) — give it a manga/anime/book title and it tells you what % of the vocabulary you already know, so you pick things that are challenging but not a wall.
**Gate:** 70-80% comprehension of a simple native manga chapter unaided, 90-95% on N3 mocks across 2-3 attempts.

## Phase 4 — past N3, toward the "watch/read natively" goal

Since you said "0 to advance": past N3, the ratio flips — structured study drops to maybe 20-30% of your time, immersion takes the rest. Imabi and JLPT Tango N2/N1 decks become as-needed references rather than a front-to-back course. This is genuinely where goal #3 (consuming native media without help) stops being aspirational — not because you'll know every word, but because your brain starts parsing structure in real time instead of translating. N2 is generally the realistic bar for working in a Japanese office, so treat it as your next real waypoint past N3.

## The four practice tracks, quickly

- **Reading:** the progression above (Tadoku → NHK Easy → Satori free tier → native manga via the legal Japanese apps → light novels).
- **Writing:** two separate skills — handwriting (genkouyoushi + stroke order) and composition (daily journaling on HelloTalk/Tandem, asking *why* on corrections, not just accepting the fix).
- **Listening:** learn to **shadow** — play a clip you mostly understand, listen once, then play it again while speaking along *simultaneously*, matching rhythm and pitch, 3-5 reps per clip. Start with NHK Easy audio (slow, clear, free). This trains mouth and ear together, not just comprehension.
- **JLPT specifically:** [jlpt.jp's official sample workbooks](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/samples/sampleindex.html) are the gold standard (literally from the test-makers, free PDF, all levels) — do one untimed early just to learn the format, then move to full timed mocks via japanesetest4you.com and passjapanese.com once you're 60-70% through a phase. Track section scores individually, not just totals — that sectional-minimum trap is real. And there's no negative marking, so never leave a blank.

## 🎯 The Core Method (read this first)

Almost every self-learner who reaches real fluency in Japanese — not just exam-passing ability — ends up combining **two engines** run in parallel from week one, not sequentially:

1. **Structured input (the "spine"):** grammar explanations + SRS (spaced repetition) for kanji/vocab. This is what gets you accuracy, exam scores, and the ability to *notice* what you're hearing/reading.
2. **Comprehensible immersion (the "flesh"):** listening/reading real (or near-real) Japanese from very early on, even before you understand most of it. This is what actually builds the intuitive, native-like processing speed that lets you watch anime or read a novel *without* mentally translating.

People who only do #1 end up able to pass exams but freeze up watching real Japanese. People who only do #2 (rare, but it happens) pick up fluency slowly and inefficiently without a way to fill gaps. **You're going to do both, every single week, from Phase 0 onward.** The phases below aren't "grammar phase, then immersion phase" — immersion starts in Phase 1 at a comprehension level near 0%, and structured study continues all the way to N2/N1. Only the *ratio* shifts over time (more structured study early, more immersion later).

The retention backbone for the structured side is **spaced repetition** — specifically **Anki**, which is free, open-source, and (since v23.10) has a built-in modern scheduler called **FSRS** that reduces your daily review load by roughly 18–31% compared to the old default algorithm for the same retention. Turn it on immediately: `Deck Options → Advanced → Optimizer → FSRS`.

## 📊 Recalibrating "90–95%" (read before you touch N5)

This matters more than it looks: **the JLPT is not scored as a percentage, and there's no letter grade.** It's pure pass/fail, and passing requires clearing *two* separate bars:

1. Your **total score** must be at or above the overall pass mark.
2. Your score in **every individual section** must independently clear that section's minimum — score zero-worthy on Listening alone and you fail the whole test, no matter how high your total is.

| Level | Total points | Overall pass mark | ~Pass % | Section minimums |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N5 | 180 | 80 | ~44% | Language Knowledge+Reading: 38/120 · Listening: 19/60 |
| N4 | 180 | 90 | ~50% | Language Knowledge+Reading: 38/120 · Listening: 19/60 |
| N3 | 180 | 95 | ~53% | Vocab/Grammar: 19/60 · Reading: 19/60 · Listening: 19/60 |

So the exam itself only asks for low-to-mid 40s–50s percent overall. **You're aiming for 90–95%**, which is nearly double the actual pass bar. That's the right instinct if your goal is *real ability*, not just a certificate — but it means your target for each phase is:

> [!success] What "90–95%" means in practice
> Consistently scoring 90%+ **raw accuracy** (not scaled score) on **full, timed, official-format mock exams**, in **every section**, across at least 2–3 separate attempts before you consider a level "done." Not one lucky run — consistency across sections is what proves you actually know the material rather than having crammed a subset of it.

One more practical fact: **the JLPT has no negative marking.** Never leave a bubble blank — a blank answer is a guaranteed zero, a guess is a 25% chance at a point.

## 🗺️ The Five Phases

### Phase 0 — Kana (absolute zero)

**Goal:** Instant, hesitation-free recognition of all hiragana and katakana (46 base characters each + dakuten/handakuten + combination sounds).

**How:**
1. Learn hiragana using a mnemonic method (Tofugu's free hiragana guide is the standard approach — a memorable little story/image per character). Don't use romaji as a crutch past week one.
2. Drill with a quiz tool daily (Real Kana, or a simple Anki kana deck) until each character is instant.
3. Repeat for katakana once hiragana is solid — katakana is genuinely harder to retain since you'll see it less often early on, so don't shortcut it.

**Time:** 1–2 weeks of daily 20–30 minute drilling is realistic for most learners.

> [!success] ✅ Validation gate — move to Phase 1 when:
> You can read **any** hiragana or katakana character (including dakuten/combinations like きゃ, ぱ, ヴ) correctly within 1–2 seconds, no sounding-out, no romaji lookup. Test yourself with a randomized quiz, not the memorized order of the chart.

---

### Phase 1 — Foundations (N5)

**Grammar:** Start Tae Kim's Guide from the beginning, in parallel with Cure Dolly's YouTube series (watch a video, then read the matching Tae Kim section — they reinforce each other well since both use the same "understand it in Japanese terms" approach).

**Vocab/Kanji:** Install Anki, turn on FSRS, import **Kaishi 1.5k**. Start with 10–15 new cards/day — resist the urge to start higher; the reviews compound fast.

**Immersion (yes, already):** Watch Comprehensible Japanese's absolute-beginner playlist. You will understand almost nothing at first — that's expected and fine. The point is training your ear to the sounds/rhythm of the language before you're being tested on comprehension.

**Reading:** Skim (don't fully read) an NHK Easy News headline once a day, just to see real Japanese in the wild.

**JLPT:** Once you're 6–8 weeks in, do one official N5 sample section untimed, just to see the format — not to score yourself yet.

> [!success] ✅ Validation gate — move to Phase 2 when:
> - You've completed at least one full pass through Kaishi 1.5k (reviews ongoing is fine, they never fully "finish").
> - You've read through Tae Kim's grammar guide's early chapters / Cure Dolly's core playlist.
> - You score **90%+ across every section** on a full, timed, official-format N5 mock exam — not just overall, each section individually.

---

### Phase 2 — Building (N4)

**Grammar:** Continue Tae Kim into intermediate territory; cross-reference anything unclear in Imabi. If you want a second explanation style, this is a good point to sample Japanese Ammo with Misa.

**Vocab/Kanji:** Add the **JLPT Tango N4** deck alongside your ongoing Kaishi reviews.

**Immersion:** Move into **Tadoku Levels 0–2**. Start mixing in slightly more native-adjacent content — simple vlogs, children's shows, more advanced Comprehensible Japanese videos.

**Reading:** Move from skimming to actually reading full NHK Easy articles, furigana on. Aim for a few per week.

**Writing:** Get your first genkouyoushi sheet and start writing basic kanji you're learning, with correct stroke order. Create a HelloTalk profile and post your first short journal entries in Japanese — native speakers will correct them for free.

**Listening:** Introduce **shadowing** (see the Listening deep-dive below) using NHK Easy's audio.

**JLPT:** Work japanesetest4you.com's N4 sections into your weekly routine.

> [!success] ✅ Validation gate — move to Phase 3 when:
> - You can read a **Tadoku Level 2** story with only occasional dictionary lookups.
> - You score **90%+ across every section** on a full, timed N4 mock exam, consistently across 2 attempts.

---

### Phase 3 — Consolidation (N3)

**Grammar:** This is where Imabi shifts from "occasional reference" to a real primary source, since N3 grammar starts getting nuanced (distinguishing near-synonym grammar points, formal register, etc.). This is also the point where Bunpro (if you choose to use it) earns its keep — its N3 grammar coverage and cloze-SRS format are excellent for exactly this kind of nuance work.

**Vocab/Kanji:** Add the **JLPT Tango N3** deck. Consider layering in Core 6000 for broader coverage if your daily review capacity allows it.

**Immersion:** Push into **Tadoku Levels 3–5**, use **Satori Reader's free tier** fully (its N4–N2 range overlaps perfectly with where you are now), and start your first real **sentence mining**: install Yomitan, browse simple native web content or an easy manga chapter (via Shonen Jump+/Comic DAYS/etc.), and one-click export unknown words straight into a personal Anki deck.

**Reading:** Begin the actual transition to native manga — pick a series with simple, everyday dialogue (slice-of-life tends to be easier than fantasy/battle manga, which invents vocabulary). Use jpdb.io to check your expected coverage % before committing to a title.

**Writing:** Keep journaling on HelloTalk/Tandem, now writing longer entries and asking for corrections on *why* something is wrong, not just what the fix is.

**Listening:** Expand shadowing to native podcasts. Start a rotation of 2–3 shows/podcasts you actually enjoy, even if comprehension is partial.

**JLPT:** Full official N3 sample sets + japanesetest4you.com + passjapanese.com mock exams, timed.

> [!success] ✅ Validation gate — move to Phase 4 when:
> - You can get through a simple native manga chapter understanding roughly 70–80% unaided (the rest via Yomitan lookups).
> - You score **90–95% across every section** on full, timed N3 mock exams, consistently across 2–3 attempts.

### Phase 4 — Breakthrough (N2 → N1 → True Native Fluency)

You asked for "0 to advance," so here's the honest shape of what comes after N3 — lighter detail since your explicit exam targets stopped at N3, but this is where the *native media without any help* goal (your #3) actually gets fully realized.

- **The ratio flips.** By now structured study should be maybe 20–30% of your time, immersion 70–80% — the reverse of Phase 1.
- **Grammar:** Imabi and (optionally) Bunpro's N2/N1 tracks become reference tools you consult when something native content throws at you doesn't parse, rather than a course you work through front-to-back.
- **Vocab:** JLPT Tango N2/N1 decks plus Core 6000 give you structure if you want it; jpdb.io's per-title decks become more useful than generic decks, since at this stage "the vocabulary in the specific show/book I'm consuming" matters more than "the vocabulary a textbook thinks is important."
- **Immersion becomes the main event:** full anime with Japanese subtitles (not English — Japanese subs reinforce reading+listening together), light novels, native YouTube on topics you actually care about, native podcasts at normal speed.
- **This is also roughly where "read/watch without help" (your goal #3) stops being aspirational and starts being real** — not because you'll know every word (native speakers don't either), but because your brain will be parsing structure and meaning in real time instead of translating.

N2 is generally the realistic bar for office-level work in Japan, so treat it as the natural next waypoint past N3 — N3 → N2 is mostly volume: more of everything above, sustained over more months, plus deliberately harder immersion content pushing your edges.

## 📖 Reading — Full Practice Track

Progression, start to finish:

1. **Kana era:** NHK Easy headlines (skim only), Tadoku Level 0.
2. **N5–N4:** Tadoku Levels 0–2, full NHK Easy articles, furigana on.
3. **N3:** Tadoku Levels 3–5, Satori Reader free tier, furigana toggled off increasingly often (force yourself to read kanji without the crutch once you recognize it 80%+ of the time).
4. **N3→native:** Simple native manga via Shonen Jump+/Comic DAYS/Magazine Pocket/Sunday Webry/GANMA! (all free, official, raw Japanese) — use jpdb.io's coverage calculator to pick titles that are challenging but not overwhelming (aim for 85%+ known vocabulary as your "ready" threshold, per jpdb's own recommended standard).
5. **Native:** Light novels, native web articles, whatever genuinely interests you — interest matters more than "level" once you're here, since motivation is what keeps volume up.

**Daily habit that matters more than any single resource:** read *something* in Japanese every day, even five minutes. Reading is the skill that degrades fastest without regular contact.

## ✍️ Writing — Full Practice Track

Two separate skills live under "writing" — keep them separate in your practice:

**A) Handwriting (stroke order, kanji forms):**
- Genkouyoushi sheets (free generators linked above), one character per box, four quadrants guiding stroke placement.
- Practice new kanji from your Anki reviews by hand, not just recognition — recognition and production are different skills, and typing-only study leaves production weak (a common complaint among self-learners who never touch a pen).

**B) Composition (expressing yourself in sentences):**
- Start a simple daily-life journal in Japanese as soon as you know basic sentence patterns (end of Phase 1 is realistic) — even three sentences a day.
- Post it on HelloTalk or Tandem and ask for corrections. Don't just accept the fix — ask *why* when you don't understand the correction; that's where the actual learning happens.
- As you progress, try writing about more abstract topics (opinions, plans, summaries of things you read) — this is what actually gets tested in N3+ writing-adjacent tasks and, more importantly, in real communication.

## 🎧 Listening — Full Practice Track

**Shadowing technique** (your single highest-value listening exercise, worth explaining properly since it's less obvious how to do than "just listen more"):
1. Pick short audio you mostly understand (NHK Easy audio is ideal to start).
2. Listen once through for comprehension.
3. Play it again — this time speak along *simultaneously*, matching rhythm, pitch, and pronunciation as closely as possible, even if you fumble words.
4. Repeat the same clip 3–5 times. You're training your mouth and ear together, not just your comprehension.

**Progression:**
1. **Phase 1:** Comprehensible Japanese absolute-beginner playlist — pure exposure, no pressure to understand.
2. **Phase 2:** Add NHK Easy audio shadowing; branch into other CI channels (Nihongo-doki, Yuyu Japanese) for voice variety.
3. **Phase 3:** Native podcasts on rotation (pick 2–3 you enjoy, even partial comprehension), continue shadowing with slightly harder material.
4. **Phase 4:** Anime with Japanese subtitles (not English), native podcasts at natural speed, drama/variety shows.

## 🎯 JLPT Exam-Specific Practice — Full Track

1. **Know the format cold before you grind content.** Do one full official sample set (untimed) the moment you start a level, purely to internalize question types and timing — this alone measurably improves scores independent of language ability.
2. **Practice in full, timed mock exams, not just isolated drill questions**, once you're 60–70% through a phase's content. Isolated drills (japanesetest4you.com) build the raw skill; full mocks (passjapanese.com, official jlpt.jp samples) build exam stamina and time management, which is a real, separate skill.
3. **Respect the sectional-minimum trap.** Because each section needs its own passing score, don't let a strong Reading score convince you to neglect Listening (or vice versa) — track your section scores individually on every mock, not just your total.
4. **Never leave a blank.** No negative marking — always fill in every bubble.
5. **Target: 90–95% raw accuracy, every section, 2–3 consecutive mocks**, per the recalibration above, before you consider a level genuinely "done" rather than merely "passable."

## 🏫 "Learn Tomorrow's Lesson Tonight" Workflow

Since you'll be in a language school and self-studying ahead of class, here's how to make that efficient rather than duplicated effort:

1. **Identify your textbook.** The overwhelming majority of language schools with a Japan-work/study track (which is almost certainly your school's orientation) use **Minna no Nihongo** — it's the global standard for practical, conversation-first instruction, as opposed to Genki, which is more common in university academic settings. Confirm which one your school uses in week one.
2. **If it's Minna no Nihongo:** free lesson-by-lesson grammar and vocabulary breakdowns exist online, mapped exactly to the textbook's 50 lessons — read the site's breakdown of tomorrow's lesson number tonight, before class. ([learnjapaneseaz.com/minna-no-nihongo](https://learnjapaneseaz.com/minna-no-nihongo) has all 50 lessons broken down for free; there are also free lesson-by-lesson YouTube walkthroughs — search "Minna no Nihongo Lesson [number]".)
3. **If it's Genki (or you want a second pass regardless):** Bunpro's free trial/paid tier lets you follow grammar in Genki's exact chapter order, which functions the same way.
4. **Either way, the workflow is:** tonight → skim tomorrow's lesson number in the free breakdown → note 2–3 unfamiliar grammar points → look them up in Tae Kim/Imabi for the deeper "why" → walk into class already recognizing the shape of what's being taught, so class time reinforces instead of introduces. This is exactly how you turn a language school into acceleration rather than a pace-setting bottleneck.

## ⚠️ Common Pitfalls (worth reading once)

- **Anki overload.** The #1 reason self-learners quit. Start conservative (10–15 new cards/day) and only raise it once your daily review count feels comfortable, not exciting. A pile of 300+ overdue reviews is demoralizing and avoidable.
- **Passive immersion.** Watching/listening without any active engagement (pausing, looking things up, noticing patterns) builds far less than the same time spent actively — especially once you're past the pure "ear training" phase 0–1 stage.
- **Avoiding output too long.** It's tempting to keep "consuming" (grammar, Anki, immersion) indefinitely before ever producing a sentence yourself. Start journaling in Phase 1, badly, on purpose — production ability doesn't appear automatically from enough input; it needs its own practice.
- **Treating class and self-study as separate tracks.** Feed what you learn in self-study into class discussion, and feed class corrections back into your Anki/journal. They should reinforce each other, not run in parallel without touching.
